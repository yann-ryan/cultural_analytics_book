49  Remaking the critique charts: Du Bois, from raw data (synthetic)

This dataset is synthetic

The data in this exercise was invented for teaching. It is not historical data, and you can’t use it as evidence about Georgia, its counties or the people who lived there.

Only the state totals are real: when you add up the file correctly, you get the numbers Du Bois printed on his charts. Everything below that level (the counties, the detailed categories, the missing returns, the spelling mistakes) was made up at random, to give you the kind of raw data that Du Bois and his students would have had to work from. Every row of the file says so in its note column.

This is a harder version of the Du Bois challenge. There, the numbers came already added up, one per year. Here you start from something closer to the tax records themselves: a table for each county, with property divided into twelve detailed categories, typed up by different clerks. Your job is to get from that to the numbers on Du Bois’s chart, and then to chart them.

Do it in R-Studio, in your cultural_analytics project, in a new .Rmd file.

How this exercise works. As in the other remakes: most steps don’t tell you which function to use. If you’re stuck, open the Hint. Steps marked New use something we haven’t covered, and their hint gives you the code. Numbers in brackets let you check your work.

The data

library(tidyverse)

digest <- read_csv("https://raw.githubusercontent.com/yann-ryan/cultural_analytics_book/main/data/dubois/property_by_county_SYNTHETIC.csv")

Each row is one kind of property in one county, with the assessed value in dollars of that property owned by Black Georgians, in each of six years. Some returns are missing: the cell is empty, or says n.r. (not returned).

The twelve categories belong to the four kinds of property on Du Bois’s charts:

Kind Categories
land Farm land, Woodland
town Town lots, City property
furniture Household furniture, Kitchen furniture
other Horses and mules, Cattle and hogs, Plantation and mechanical tools, Money and solvent debts, Merchandise, All other property

Part 1: from raw data to Du Bois’s numbers

The goal of this part is a table with one row per year and kind of property, and the value for the whole state: (24 rows)

year kind value
1875 furniture 21,186
… … …

1. Look at digest. What is one row? How many counties are there? (480 rows, 40 counties) Is it tidy? What would you need to change to make a chart with the year on the x axis?

2. How many different spellings of the categories are there? There should be twelve categories. (36)

Hint

count() the category column, or use n_distinct() inside summarise().

3. Clean the category names, so that there are exactly twelve. Most of the problems are capital letters and extra spaces. One isn’t. (12 categories, 40 rows each)

Hint

str_to_lower() and str_trim(), inside mutate(), fix most of it (you used str_trim() in home exercise 6). Then count() again to find the one that’s left, and fix it with if_else() or case_when().

4. Reshape the table so that there is one column for the year and one for the value. Make the year a number. (2,880 rows)

Hint

pivot_longer(). The columns are called 1875 to 1899: names that start with a number need backticks, `1875`:`1899`. Column names always become text, so turn year into a number afterwards with as.numeric().

5. New: the values are text, like "$18,313", because of the dollar sign and the commas. parse_number(), from the readr package, reads the number out of a piece of text, and ignores everything else: parse_number("$18,313") gives 18313. Use it to turn value into a number. Read the warning: what does it tell you? How many values are missing now? (40)

Hint (new)

mutate(value = parse_number(value)). The warning lists the cells that contain n.r., which can’t be read as a number. They become NA, like the empty cells.

6. Add a column called kind, with the kind of property each category belongs to, using the table above.

Hint

case_when(), with %in% for each kind: category %in% c("farm land", "woodland") ~ "land". The last line can be TRUE ~ "other", which catches everything else. Is that safe, if one of your categories is still misspelt?

7. Add up the values for the whole state, for each year and kind. (24 rows)

Hint

group_by() and summarise() with sum(). If you get NAs, look at what sum() does with missing values, and how to tell it to skip them.

8. Check your numbers against Du Bois’s chart. Furniture in 1875 should be 21,186 dollars, and in 1899, 1,434,975. If your furniture numbers are too small, go back to step 3.

9. Add each year’s total of all four kinds, and each kind’s share of that total, as a percentage. (total in 1899: 13,447,423; furniture was 0.4% of the total in 1875 and 10.7% in 1899)

Hint

This time you want to keep every row, and add the year’s total next to it: group_by(year) followed by mutate(), not summarise().

10. Discuss: 40 returns were missing. You skipped them in step 7, so they count as 0. Here, that happens to give the right answer, because we made up the data so that it would. With real records, what could you do about a missing return? Would Du Bois’s numbers have had the same problem?

Part 2: the furniture spiral

Keep only the furniture rows, and make Du Bois’s spiral from them. Follow Part 1 of the Du Bois challenge, from step 3: the hints are there.

Part 3: charting the four kinds

Follow Part 3 of the Du Bois challenge: one chart in Du Bois’s style, one in a plain style, and decide which works better. Your caption must say that the data is synthetic.

Part 4: what synthetic data can’t tell you

11. Make a chart of the value of furniture in each county in 1899, ordered from largest to smallest.

12. Discuss: the chart looks like a finding: some counties look much richer than others. What does it actually show? What could you learn from real county-level data that you can’t learn from the state totals, and what would you need to know about the counties to make sense of it (think of Report 1, and the data sources page)?
