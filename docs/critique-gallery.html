Critique gallery

Learning to make good data visualisations goes hand in hand with learning to read them critically. In four sessions of the course, you’ll spend part of the class critiquing a visualisation in a small group. Each group is given one of the six visualisations below, and keeps the same one all the way through. Each time, you come back to it with a new set of questions, based on what we’ve covered that week.

How it works:

  • In the first round, each group is given a number from 1 to 6. That’s your visualisation for the rest of the course.
  • Spend a few minutes looking at it properly before you start talking. Open it full screen, zoom in, and read the small print.
  • Discuss the questions for that round. Not every question will apply to every visualisation. Where one doesn’t, say why.
  • Choose one person to sum up your group’s discussion for the rest of the class, in two or three minutes.

This is also good practice for the exam, which includes a question asking you to critique a chart (see Preparing for the Exam).

Round 1: data and aesthetics

Tue 10 Nov, with Data Visualisation Basics and More chart types.

  1. What is one observation in this visualisation: a person, a month, a country, a film? What are the variables?
  2. What type is each variable: categorical, ordinal, discrete, or continuous?
  3. Which aesthetic is each variable mapped to (position, size, colour, shape, …)? Is anything mapped to more than one aesthetic?
  4. What kind of chart is it? Using the cards in Choosing a chart, what is it trying to show: amounts, a ranking, a distribution, a relationship, change over time, part-to-whole, a comparison, or something spatial?
  5. Are the scales used consistently? Could you read off an actual value if you wanted to?
  6. What is the main message, in one sentence? Is the chart effective at getting it across?

Round 2: perception and design

Thu 12 Nov, with Gestalt Principles.

  1. Where does your eye go first? Is that the most important thing?
  2. Think about the ‘user journey’ through the visualisation. Is it obvious what you’re meant to look at, and in what order? How long did it take you to understand how to read it?
  3. Which Gestalt principles are being used (proximity, similarity, connection, closure, enclosure, continuity)? How do they help, or get in the way?
  4. Does the design group together things that shouldn’t be grouped, or split up things that belong together?
  5. What would you remove, and what would you add, to make it easier to read?

Round 3: accessibility and ethics

Tue 24 Nov, with Ethics and data visualisation and Data Visualisation and Accessibility.

Accessibility:

  1. Run a screenshot of the visualisation through a colour-vision-deficiency simulator (see the accessibility chapter). Can you still tell all the colours apart? Does the meaning depend on colour alone?
  2. Is there enough contrast? Is the text big enough to read?
  3. How would the visualisation work for someone with low vision, or who uses a screen reader? As a group, write a one- or two-sentence alt text for it.

Ethics:

  1. Who made it, when, for whom, and why? Is it trying to persuade? Is that purpose open, or hidden behind an appearance of neutrality?
  2. Where does the data come from, and how was it collected? Who or what is counted, and who or what is missing? Is the creator open about this?
  3. What role does emotion play? Does the design make you feel something about the data?
  4. What do you imagine is the positionality of the person or people who made it? What is yours, and how does it affect how you read it?
  5. Pick one item from the ethics checklist (for example, ‘show uncertainty’ or ‘make the invisible visible’). How well does the visualisation do on it?

Round 4: maps as arguments, and a verdict

Thu 26 Nov, with Maps as data visualisation.

  1. How does location matter in your visualisation? If it’s a map: what does each area or point stand for, and what has been left off? If it isn’t: would a map show something it misses, or would it hide something it shows? (Minard’s chart is a bit of both!)
  2. For the maps (4 and 6): what is each area coloured by, and who decided the categories or the breaks? Do large areas get more visual weight than they deserve?
  3. Look back at your notes from the other three rounds. As a group, write a short critique of the visualisation, in four sentences:
    • what it shows, and for whom
    • one thing it does well, and why
    • one weakness, and why
    • one concrete change you would make, and how you would do it with ggplot2 (name the geom, scale, or other function)

This last step is exactly what the exam critique question asks you to do.
