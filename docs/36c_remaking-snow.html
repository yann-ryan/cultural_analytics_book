47  Remaking the critique charts: Snow

In this exercise you’ll remake John Snow’s map of the 1854 cholera outbreak in Soho, London, with ggplot2, and then use the data to test his argument: that the outbreak came from the water pump in Broad Street. First you’ll draw the map, then work out which pump was nearest to each death, and finally draw the outbreak over time.

Do it in R-Studio, in your cultural_analytics project, in a new .Rmd file. Keep the Snow sheet (or the full-size image) open as you go.

How this exercise works. Most steps use things you have done before, and don’t tell you which function to use. Try to work it out first, looking back at earlier chapters if you need to. If you’re stuck, open the Hint under the step. Steps marked New use something we haven’t covered: you can try to figure it out, but if you have no idea where to start, open the hint, which gives you the code. Numbers in brackets let you check your work.

The map is square, so put fig.width=8, fig.height=8 in the options of your map chunks: ```{r, fig.width=8, fig.height=8}.

The data

The geographer Waldo Tobler digitised Snow’s map in 1994: every death, every pump and every street, as x and y coordinates measured on the map. Michael Friendly later added Snow’s table of cases by date.

Copy this into the first chunk of your .Rmd and run it:

library(tidyverse)

deaths  <- read_csv("https://raw.githubusercontent.com/yann-ryan/cultural_analytics_book/main/data/snow/snow_deaths.csv")
pumps   <- read_csv("https://raw.githubusercontent.com/yann-ryan/cultural_analytics_book/main/data/snow/snow_pumps.csv")
streets <- read_csv("https://raw.githubusercontent.com/yann-ryan/cultural_analytics_book/main/data/snow/snow_streets.csv")
dates   <- read_csv("https://raw.githubusercontent.com/yann-ryan/cultural_analytics_book/main/data/snow/snow_dates.csv")
  • deaths: one row per death on the map, with its position (x, y). Where several people died in the same house, Snow drew the deaths as a stack of bars, and the points are stacked in the same way. (578 rows)
  • pumps: the 13 water pumps, with their names.
  • streets: the streets, as lines. Each street is a few rows, one for each point along it, with a number in street saying which street the point belongs to.
  • dates: Snow’s table of fatal cases by day: the number of people who fell ill (attacks) and who died (deaths) on each day. Everyone in this table died; attacks is the day their illness began.

Part 1: the map

Each layer of this map uses a different dataset. So far you have put the dataset in ggplot(), and every layer has used it. Here, leave ggplot() empty and give each layer its own: geom_point(data = deaths, aes(...)).

1. New: start with the streets. geom_path() joins points in the order they appear in the data. Draw streets with geom_path(), in grey, with one line for each street. Try it without group first: what goes wrong?

Hint (new) 
ggplot() +
  geom_path(data = streets, aes(x = x, y = y, group = street), colour = "grey60")

2. Add the deaths, as small points.

3. Add the pumps, as points that look different from the deaths (a different colour, shape and size), and label each pump with its name, just above the point.

Hint

Two more layers, both with data = pumps. Shape, colour and size go outside aes(), because they are the same for every pump: shape = 17 is a triangle. For the names, geom_text() with label inside aes(). vjust moves text up or down; try vjust = -1.

4. Find the Broad Street pump. Compare your map with Snow’s: what’s the same, and what’s different? Snow drew a stack of small bars for each house: what can you see in his version that is harder to see with points?

5. Save the map as snow_map, so you can add to it.

6. New: the x and y coordinates are in the same units, so a step to the right should be the same distance as a step up. Add coord_fixed(), then make the plot window narrower and wider. What does coord_fixed() keep the same?

7. The axes, their numbers and the grey background don’t mean anything to a reader of this map. Remove them all at once, with a preset theme.

Hint

Look at the list of preset themes linked in Chart Themes. One of them is completely empty.

Part 2: which pump was nearest?

Snow’s argument was that the deaths cluster around one pump. We can test that with the data, by working out which pump was nearest to each death.

8. pumps and deaths both have columns called x and y, which will clash when we put them together. Make a copy of pumps in which they are called pump_x and pump_y, and save it as pumps2.

Hint

rename(), from the box at the end of select(). The new name comes first.

9. New: we want every death paired with every pump, so that we can measure the distance between them. cross_join() does this: it joins two datasets with no key, putting every row of one next to every row of the other. Do it with deaths and pumps2, and save the result as pairs. How many rows does it have, and why? (7,514)

Hint (new)

pairs <- deaths |> cross_join(pumps2)

10. Add a column called distance. The straight-line distance between two points is the square root of (the difference in x, squared, plus the difference in y, squared). In R, the square root is sqrt(), and “squared” is ^2.

Hint

mutate(distance = sqrt((x - pump_x)^2 + (y - pump_y)^2))

11. For each death (case), keep only the row with the smallest distance. Save the result as nearest. (578 rows)

Hint

You’ve kept the top or bottom rows of each group before, with group_by() and one of the slice_ functions.

12. Count the deaths by nearest pump, and make a bar chart: ordered, and turned so that the pump names are readable. (Broad St: 359)

Hint

count(), then reorder() inside aes(), and coord_flip(), as in data challenge 3.

13. Go back to the map. Add a column to nearest called broad_street, which is TRUE if the nearest pump is Broad Street and FALSE if not. Use nearest instead of deaths for the deaths layer, and colour the points by broad_street.

Hint

mutate(broad_street = pump == "Broad St") gives TRUE or FALSE for each row. The colour goes inside aes() in the deaths layer.

14. Make the Broad Street deaths stand out, and everything else grey or black. The legend says TRUE and FALSE, which means nothing to a reader: change the legend labels, and give the legend a title.

Hint

scale_colour_manual(), as on Tuesday. As well as values, it takes labels, and both can be named: labels = c("TRUE" = "Broad Street", "FALSE" = ...). The legend title goes in labs(), under the name of the aesthetic.

15. Discuss: 219 people who died lived nearer to another pump. Does that weaken Snow’s argument? We measured distance in a straight line, but people walked along streets. Snow’s second version of the map drew a line around the area where Broad Street was the nearest pump on foot. How would that change your chart? What else, apart from distance, decides which pump someone used?

Part 3: the outbreak over time

The pump handle at Broad Street was removed on 8 September 1854, after Snow showed his evidence to the local parish authorities. Did that end the outbreak?

16. Look at dates. The last row has no date. Think about what it means, then remove it. (43 rows)

17. We want one line for attacks and one for deaths, in different colours. Get the data into the right shape. (86 rows)

Hint

To map colour to “attacks or deaths”, that has to be one column: the same reshaping you did for Minard.

18. Draw a line chart, with the date on the x axis and a colour for attacks and for deaths.

19. Put a break on the x axis every week, labelled like “04 Sep”.

Hint

scale_x_date(), from Tuesday. The codes for the day and the short month name are in Date scales.

20. Mark the day the pump handle was removed with a vertical dashed line, and label it. To use a date as a position on the x axis, write it as as.Date("1854-09-08").

Hint

Two annotate() layers, from Annotations: a "segment" (with x, xend, y and yend) and a "text".

21. Add a title, axis labels, a legend title and a caption with the source.

22. Discuss: look at where your line for the pump handle falls. When was the outbreak at its worst? What had already happened by 8 September? Write the sentence you would put in the subtitle, and make sure the chart supports it. Is this a weakness in Snow’s argument, or in the way it’s usually told?

23. Discuss: the table has 616 deaths in total, and the map has 578. Why might the two not match? Which number would you put in your title?
