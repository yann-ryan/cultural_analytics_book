37  Data challenge 3 (alternative): How has music changed over time?

This is an alternative to data challenge 3. It practises the same skills on a different dataset: about 30,000 songs from Spotify. Do whichever one you like (or both). You’ll use everything from the last two weeks: count(), group_by() and summarise(), ggplot() with geom_col(), geom_line(), geom_point(), geom_histogram() and geom_boxplot(), aesthetics inside and outside aes(), facet_wrap(), position = 'fill', position scales, reorder() and coord_flip().

Do it in R-Studio, in your cultural_analytics project, in a new .Rmd file. Each step gives a number in brackets, or tells you what you should see, so you can check your work.

Work through it in order. Parts 1 and 2 need little more than a count() before each chart. Parts 3 and 4 need more wrangling, and the steps marked Harder are a stretch. Get as far as you can: it’s fine not to finish.

The data

In 2019 the data scientist Kaylin Pavlik wanted to know whether a computer could tell musical genres apart just from how songs sound. She searched Spotify for playlists in six genres (EDM, Latin, pop, R&B, rap and rock, each split into four subgenres), and downloaded every song on 471 of them. For each song, Spotify’s API gave her its release date, its popularity, and a set of audio features: numbers that Spotify’s own algorithms calculate from the recording. The dataset was shared through TidyTuesday in January 2020.

There is one row per song on a playlist. The columns you’ll use most are:

  • track_name, track_artist, track_id: the song, who it’s by, and Spotify’s ID for it.
  • playlist_genre and playlist_subgenre: the genre of the playlist the song was found on.
  • track_album_release_date: when the album the song is on came out.
  • track_popularity: Spotify’s popularity score in 2020, from 0 to 100.
  • duration_ms: the length of the song, in milliseconds.
  • danceability, energy and valence: scores from 0 to 1. Valence is how positive (happy, cheerful) a song sounds: a high score is happy, a low score sad or angry.
  • loudness: the average loudness, in decibels. The values are negative: the closer to 0, the louder.
  • tempo: the speed, in beats per minute.

Setting up

You don’t need to download anything: read_csv() can read a file straight from a web address. Copy this code into the first chunk of your .Rmd and run it. It adds two columns: year (the first four characters of the release date, as a number) and duration_min (the length in minutes).

library(tidyverse)

spotify <- read_csv("https://raw.githubusercontent.com/rfordatascience/tidytuesday/main/data/2020/2020-01-21/spotify_songs.csv")

spotify <- spotify |>
  mutate(year = as.numeric(str_sub(track_album_release_date, 1, 4)),
         duration_min = duration_ms / 60000)

spotify should have 32,833 rows. If your internet is slow, save a copy in your project folder with write_csv(spotify, "spotify_songs.csv"), and read that next time.

Most release dates are written in full (2019-06-14), but some are just a year (2012). Why does str_sub() work for both? (1,855 dates are just a year)

Part 1: genres and artists

  • Count the rows by playlist_genre, and make a bar chart of the result. Use reorder() to put the bars in order of size. (edm: 6,043)

  • Count the rows by playlist_genre and playlist_subgenre (put both inside count()). How many subgenres are there? (24) Make a bar chart with the subgenres on the x axis, flipped with coord_flip() and ordered with reorder(). Then map fill to playlist_genre. Which is the biggest subgenre? (progressive electro house: 1,809)

  • The subgenres of each genre are scattered across the chart, but you can still pick them out. Which Gestalt principle lets you do that?

  • Who are the artists with the most songs in the dataset? Count the rows by track_artist, keep the top 15 with slice_max(n, n = 15), and save the result as top_artists. Make an ordered, flipped bar chart. (Martin Garrix: 161)

  • Is each artist tied to one genre? Filter spotify to the artists in top_artists (use %in% with top_artists$track_artist), then count by track_artist and playlist_genre. Make a flipped bar chart with fill = playlist_genre, then add position = 'fill' to geom_col(). Which three artists are almost entirely in one genre, and which artist is spread most evenly? (Queen: 134 of 136 songs are rock; Drake’s biggest genre has only 36 of his 100 songs)

Part 2: songs over time

  • A song can be on more than one playlist. How many different songs are there? Use n_distinct() on track_id. (28,356) Which song appears most often? Count by track_id, track_name and track_artist, and sort the result. In how many genres does it appear? (10 rows, in five genres)

  • When we count songs over time, a song that appears on ten playlists would be counted ten times. So we’ll keep just one row per song. Run this code:

songs <- spotify |>
  distinct(track_id, .keep_all = TRUE)

distinct() keeps one row for each track_id, and .keep_all = TRUE keeps all the other columns too. songs should have 28,356 rows. Each song now has only the genre of the first playlist it was found on. Use songs from now on.

  • Count songs by year, and draw it as a bar chart. Which year has the most songs? (2019: 7,406) How many songs came out in 2010 or later? (20,460, almost three-quarters) What are the oldest songs in the dataset? (two, from 1957)

  • Why do you think so few old songs are in the dataset? Think about how it was made: who makes playlists, and when were these playlists collected?

  • Did the genres change over time? Add a decade column to songs with mutate(decade = floor(year / 10) * 10), and keep only the decades from 1970 to 2010. Save the result as decades. (27,595 rows) Count decades by decade and playlist_genre, and make a bar chart with fill = playlist_genre and position = 'fill'. Use scale_x_continuous() to put a break at each decade. (rock: 89% of songs from the 1970s, 7% from the 2010s)

  • Keep that chart in mind. If we compare songs from the 1970s with songs from the 2010s, what are we really comparing?

Part 3: what does each genre sound like?

  • Draw a histogram of duration_min from songs, with binwidth = 0.25. What is the median length of a song? (3.6 minutes) Find the shortest and longest songs with slice_min() and slice_max(). (4 seconds; 8.6 minutes)

  • Which genre is the most danceable? Make a boxplot with playlist_genre on the x axis and danceability on the y axis. Put the boxes in order with reorder(playlist_genre, danceability): this orders them by the average danceability of each genre. Then calculate the median for each genre with group_by() and summarise(). (rap: 0.734; rock: 0.522)

  • Are loud songs energetic? Make a scatterplot with loudness on the x axis and energy on the y axis. With 28,000 points it’s just a blob, so set alpha = 0.1. Should alpha go inside or outside aes()? Describe the relationship in one sentence.

  • Add facet_wrap(~ playlist_genre) to the scatterplot. Which genre’s points sit furthest to the top right? Check your answer with the median energy of each genre. (edm: 0.838)

Part 4: are songs getting shorter?

Music journalists often say that songs are getting shorter, because streaming services pay per play, and listeners skip. Does this dataset agree?

  • Filter songs to the years 1970 to 2019 (2020 had only just started when the data was collected). Then use group_by(year) and summarise() to make two columns: songs = n() and median_duration = median(duration_min). Save the result as durations.

  • Draw median_duration as a line chart with points on top. Use scale_x_continuous() to put a break every five years. In which year were songs longest, and how long were they in 2019? (1990: 4.6 minutes; 2019: 3.2)

  • Now add scale_y_continuous(limits = c(0, 5)). How does the drop look now? Which version of the chart would you use, and why?

  • Remember Part 2: the old songs are mostly rock, and the new ones aren’t. Maybe songs are only getting shorter because rock songs are long? Use decades to calculate the median duration (and the number of songs, with n()) for each decade and playlist_genre. Draw a line chart with decade on the x axis and colour = playlist_genre. Are songs from the 2010s shorter than songs from the 1990s in every genre? Which genre changed most? (yes; rap went from 4.4 to 3.3 minutes)

  • One point on that chart is much higher than the rest. Look at its number of songs. Should we trust it? (edm in the 1970s: 7.6 minutes, from 4 songs)

  • Harder: has the sound of music changed too? Starting from songs filtered to 1970–2019, use group_by(year) and summarise() to calculate the median loudness, valence and danceability for each year. Use pivot_longer() to put the three medians in one column, and draw a line chart with facet_wrap(), one panel per feature. Try scales = "free_y": why do you need it here? In which year were songs loudest, and when were they least happy? (loudest: 2013; lowest valence: 2014) Look up the “loudness war”.

Write a short paragraph (four or five sentences) about popular music, based on your charts. Pick the chart you think tells the most interesting story, and say what it shows. Then think about the data. These songs come from playlists one person found on Spotify in late 2019, in six genres she chose. The audio features come from Spotify’s own algorithms, which aren’t public (and which Spotify stopped sharing with new apps in 2024). The year is when the album came out, not the song. How might each of these things affect what your chart shows?

Bonus

  • Draw a histogram of track_popularity with binwidth = 1. What’s strange about it? (2,620 songs have a popularity of 0) Look at a few of them. Why might a well-known song get a 0? (Hint: remember that each version of a song has its own track_id.) Which is the most popular song? (Dance Monkey: 100)

  • Filter songs to EDM, and draw a histogram of tempo with binwidth = 1. One tempo is far more common than any other. Which is it? (128 beats per minute: 1,526 songs)

  • The mode column is 1 for songs in a major key and 0 for a minor key. Use mutate() and if_else() to turn it into "major" and "minor", then make a bar chart with playlist_genre on the x axis, fill = mode, and position = 'fill'. Which genre has the most songs in a major key? (rock: 69%)
